## First, how I feel about it

Disappointed? Yes. Defeated? No.

Getting rejected is part of building something new. My job as a founder is not to avoid problems. My job is to fix them, stay hopeful and be ready for the next one, because there will always be a next one.

## Is it normal for Apple to reject your app?

Yes. Very normal.

In its [2025 App Store Transparency Report](https://www.apple.com/legal/app-store/transparency/2025), Apple says it reviewed 9,100,620 app submissions and rejected 2,093,244 of them. That's about 23%, or roughly 1 in 4.

![Bar chart showing Apple reviewed 9.1 million app submissions in 2025 and rejected 2.1 million, about 1 in 4](/images/uploads/apple-rejects-your-app-chart.jpg)

Big companies get rejected. Funded startups get rejected. Founders with full engineering teams get rejected.

Here's the part most people never hear. On its [App Review page](https://developer.apple.com/distribute/app-review/), Apple says over 40% of unresolved issues come from 1 rule: guideline 2.1, called App Completeness. In plain words, Apple wants a finished app. No crashes, no dead buttons, no "coming soon" screens, and a way for the reviewer to log in and test everything.

That means many rejections come down to details. And details can be fixed.

## What to do when Apple rejects your app: 5 steps

### 1. Read the full message before you react

Open App Store Connect (Apple's website where you manage your app) and go to the App Review section. Apple's message will name the guideline your app broke, usually as a number like 2.1 or 5.1.1.

Read it twice. Send it to your developer. Don't guess what Apple meant. Work from what they actually wrote.

### 2. Look up the exact guideline

Every guideline number links back to Apple's [App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/). Read that whole section, not just the line in your message.

This is where AI saves me hours. I paste the rejection message and the guideline into [Claude](https://claude.ai) and ask: "Explain this in plain English, and give me a checklist of everything in my app that could trigger it." In 2 minutes I have a clear fix list instead of a headache.

### 3. Fix the exact issue, then check the usual suspects

Fix what Apple named first. Then scan your app for the other common problems, so you don't get sent back for something new. Apple lists the issues it sees most often, and here they are in plain English:

**Crashes and bugs.** Test every screen on a real iPhone before you submit.

**Broken links.** Tap every link, including your support and privacy links.

**Placeholder content.** Replace any test text or empty screens with the real thing.

**Incomplete information.** Give Apple a working demo account and clear notes. A reviewer who can't get in can't approve you.

**Privacy policy issues.** Link your privacy policy inside the app and on your App Store listing.

**Unclear permission requests.** If your app asks for photos, contacts or the camera, say clearly why.

**Inaccurate screenshots.** Your store images have to match the real app.

### 4. Reply or appeal if Apple got it wrong

Sometimes the reviewer simply missed something. You can reply to App Review right inside App Store Connect and explain.

If you truly believe your app follows the rules, you can appeal to the App Review Board. Apple allows 1 appeal per submission, and you need to give specific reasons. Stay calm, clear and factual.

### 5. Resubmit and update your team

Once the fix is in, resubmit. Then give your team a realistic new timeline. Which brings me to the part nobody teaches you.

## What not to do after a rejection

A rejection is a moment where small mistakes can cost you weeks. Avoid these:

**Don't resubmit the same build and hope for a different reviewer.** If nothing changed, the answer won't change either.

**Don't argue in your reply.** Reviewers are people. Clear and kind gets you further than frustrated and defensive.

**Don't rush 10 changes at once without testing.** Fixing 1 problem and creating 2 new ones is the fastest way to a second rejection.

**Don't go silent.** Your team, your creators and your early users can handle a delay. What they can't handle is not knowing.

## How I keep 30 creators excited while we wait

A rejection doesn't just delay your app. It tests your team.

My creators signed up because they believe in VIXA, and I take that seriously. Here's how I keep them close:

**Tell them first.** They should never learn about a delay from a public post. A short, honest message: what happened, what we're doing and when they'll hear from me next.

**Give them a job.** Waiting feels long when there's nothing to do. Their accounts keep warming up, and they keep practicing their hooks so they're ready on day 1.

**Show the process.** "We got rejected, here's what we're fixing" is a real story. Real stories build trust.

**Keep your word on updates.** If I said Friday, I update them Friday, even if the news is "still in review."

## How I run my creator program with AI

This is the part people ask me about the most.

I don't run my creator group alone, and I don't run it with a big team either. I run it with AI and 2 creator managers: 1 for influencers and 1 for UGC creators. (UGC means user generated content: real people making videos about your product.)

My managers bring the warmth and the human touch. AI brings the speed. It answers the same questions creators ask over and over, sends reminders, keeps 1 live list of every creator and what she still has to do, and turns my voice notes into short guides.

I don't write code. I just tell it what I need in plain words, like:

* "Answer the new creator messages."
* "Let the creators know the launch date moved, and what to work on this week."
* "Show me a list of who still needs to send her account link."

So even on a day like today, the program doesn't stop. My creators still get answers, and I get to focus on fixing the app.

## 1 thing you cannot skip

Give Apple a working demo account every single time you submit, with a short note that walks the reviewer through your main feature.

It sounds small. It isn't. If the reviewer can't log in, they can't see what your app does, and the answer will be no before they ever reach the good part. Test the login yourself the morning you submit.
