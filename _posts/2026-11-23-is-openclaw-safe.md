## What Is OpenClaw, in Plain English?

OpenClaw is an AI assistant you install on your own computer. Instead of chatting with it in a website, you text it from apps like WhatsApp, Telegram, iMessage, Discord or Slack, and it does things for you on your machine. It can run commands, control a web browser, read and write your files, manage your calendar and remember everything across conversations, according to [DigitalOcean's security breakdown](https://www.digitalocean.com/resources/articles/openclaw-security-challenges).

It was created by developer Peter Steinberger and went viral fast. It also changed names twice in a few weeks, from Clawdbot to Moltbot to OpenClaw, which [Bitsight tracked](https://www.bitsight.com/blog/openclaw-ai-security-risks-exposed-instances) as the number of live setups exploded in late January 2026.

The magic and the danger come from the same place. OpenClaw can add new abilities called skills, which you download from a public store called ClawHub. Each skill is a little piece of software someone else wrote. Think of it like letting a stranger hand your assistant a new set of instructions and the keys to your house.

Let me translate the scariest parts into normal words.

**1. Hundreds of weak spots.** A vulnerability is a crack in the software that a hacker could use to get in. 1 early audit counted 512 of them, and 8 were rated critical, the worst level. Kaspersky's verdict on trusting OpenClaw with your life was blunt: "at best unsafe, and at worst utterly reckless."

**2. Setups left wide open.** When people installed OpenClaw, many accidentally put their control panel on the public internet. Researchers found tens of thousands of these. A security researcher showed he could see API keys, chat histories and messaging tokens without logging in at all.

**3. Poisoned add-ons.** This one scares me most as a mom running businesses. Koi Security checked every skill in ClawHub and found 341 malicious ones. Many told users to install a fake "prerequisite" that was actually malware built to steal passwords from Macs.

**4. Passwords stored in plain text.** Security firm Permiso reported that OpenClaw kept credentials, like AI provider keys and messaging tokens, in plain readable text on the computer, according to [DigitalOcean](https://www.digitalocean.com/resources/articles/openclaw-security-challenges). If anything gets in, it can read them.

And here is the fact that made me put my iron down. When Bitsight tested OpenClaw, it found that the software [did not force a strong password or token at all](https://www.bitsight.com/blog/openclaw-ai-security-risks-exposed-instances). ## The Hype Lesson Every Founder Should Learn

There is 1 more story from this year that I keep thinking about, because it has nothing to do with code and everything to do with hype.

When OpenClaw blew up, a side project called Moltbook launched with it: a social network where AI agents post and talk to each other. It grew to millions of agents almost overnight. Then in early February, the cloud security company Wiz discovered that Moltbook's database had no access controls at all. According to [DigitalOcean's summary](https://www.digitalocean.com/resources/articles/openclaw-security-challenges), that exposed about 1.5 million API tokens, 35,000 email addresses and private agent messages.

Read that again. 1.5 million keys, left in an unlocked room, because everyone was moving too fast to check the lock.

As a founder who just launched an app, this hit me hard. I know the pressure to move fast and be first. But in business, the excitement of being first never protects you from the cost of being careless. Your customers do not care how viral a tool was when their information leaks. They only remember who they trusted.

So here is the rule I live by now, and I want you to steal it: **the more a tool can touch, the slower I say yes.** A tool that writes captions can be tested today. A tool that can read my inbox, my files and my customers' data has to earn it first.

That same idea shows up inside companies too. Security firm Token Security found that 22% of its business customers had employees running OpenClaw without the company's approval, according to the same DigitalOcean report. If you have even 1 contractor or assistant, ask them which AI tools they have connected to your accounts. You might be surprised.

## The Fair Side: OpenClaw Is Getting Better

I want to be fair, because I do not believe in tearing down builders. OpenClaw is free, open source and moving fast, and its team has been working hard to clean things up.

Gateway logins are now required by default. ClawHub teamed up with VirusTotal to scan new skills before people download them. And in September 2026, the security firm Trail of Bits did a full review. It sent 24 rated reports: 0 critical, 2 high, 16 medium and 6 low. The OpenClaw team says [every actionable issue has been repaired](https://openclaw.ai/blog/openclaw-trail-of-bits-engagement-recap/) and shipped in its stable releases.

That is real progress, and I respect it. But "safer than it was" is not the same as "safe for me." The public tracker still lists 211 security advisories, and the biggest risk never lived in the code alone. It lives in the setup. A tool that runs your whole computer is only as safe as the person who configured it, and Bitsight's advice was clear: it is not for most non-technical users.

Here is something most people do not know. OpenClaw is not its own AI brain. It is the body, and you plug in an AI model as the brain. Kaspersky even suggested using a Claude model inside OpenClaw because it is good at spotting tricks hidden in content. So the real question is not "OpenClaw or Claude." It is "who is guarding the door to my computer?"

## If You Still Want to Try OpenClaw, Do It Like This

I get it. It is exciting, and if you are a tinkerer, you will want to play. If you do, follow the advice from [Kaspersky](https://www.kaspersky.com/blog/openclaw-vulnerabilities-exposed/55263/) and [Bitsight](https://www.bitsight.com/blog/openclaw-ai-security-risks-exposed-instances):

1. **Use a spare computer or a separate server.** Never your main laptop, your work computer or the one your kids use.
2. **Keep it off the public internet.** Bitsight suggests a private network tool like Tailscale instead of opening it to the world.
3. **Use burner accounts.** Connect throwaway messaging and email accounts, not your real business ones.
4. **Install only skills you have checked.** If a skill asks you to download a "prerequisite," stop right there.
5. **Run the built-in security check often.** Kaspersky recommends running `security audit --deep` regularly.
6. **Never connect your bank, payroll or customer data.** Not until you would bet your business on it.

## FAQ

**Is OpenClaw safe to use in 2026?**
It is safer than it was early in the year. A September 2026 audit by Trail of Bits found 0 critical issues, and the team fixed every actionable report. But the public tracker still lists 211 security advisories, and safety depends heavily on how you set it up, so most non-technical users should not run it on their main computer.

**What is the difference between OpenClaw and Claude?**
OpenClaw is free, open source agent software that runs on your own computer and can use different AI models as its brain, including Claude. Claude is Anthropic's AI assistant, which works in the Claude app, in your browser and through connectors you approve, with security handled by Anthropic.

**Can OpenClaw steal my passwords?**
OpenClaw itself is not malware. But in February 2026, researchers found 341 malicious skills in its ClawHub store that hid password stealing malware, and earlier versions stored some credentials in plain text. Only install skills you have checked, and keep passwords in a password manager.
